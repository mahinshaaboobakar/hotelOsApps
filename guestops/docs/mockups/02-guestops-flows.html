GuestOps — the design's flows, drawn

The companion to 01-guestops-gold.html. That page is what a person sees; this one is what happens — the stay's state machine and its single rule for facts that arrive out of order, both write paths, the Integration Hub's deferred queue draining in, a room move reaching every consumer, and the five failure paths that a hotel produces every week. Everything here traces to a scenario (Sn), a PMS requirement (Rn) or an owner ruling (GUEST-Q1…Q6). Nothing is drawn that the design does not build, except where it says so.
The stay

Cancelled and No-show are business facts, not lifecycle states in the platform's sense. ADR 0062's active / deleted_at answer whether a record exists, and a cancelled reservation exists — it keeps its cancellation time, its reason and its penalty, and it stays in the bookings list (S25, S27). The verbs are Cancel and RecordNoShow, in that ADR's own idiom. The dashed arrow is the only way backwards, it takes the stay's write permission, and it is recorded as a correction — the guest who was checked out in error at 07:00 and is still asleep in the room (S24). Inbound facts are monotonic; people are not.

2 · R7's one rule. The reference answered this condition three times and removed none of them. This is the whole answer

the rule, written once
An inbound fact is applied only if its lifecycle rank is greater than or equal to the rank the stay already holds. A fact of lower rank is not applied: it is recorded as a contradiction on the stay. An antecedent that never arrived is recorded as not observed and is never synthesised.

Why one rule and not three mechanisms. The system this replaces met out-of-order arrival and answered it with per-flavour forceCheckIn flags, directCheckout fallbacks marked "Todo remove after data flow is ok", and a commented-out replay that would have injected a check-in that never happened — all live at once, with the decision record being the comments themselves (R7). The cost was not complexity; it was that nobody could say which path a given stay had taken. A single comparison on lifecycle rank answers every row above, and the rows are consequences rather than cases.

The difference is who may write, never what is stored. Both paths land in the same schema and publish the same fact. The permissions are identical too — the mode is not an authorization boundary; what changes is only whether the write is marked as an override.
There is no third path for "the connector is down". GUEST-Q4: PMS-writes-first at all times, so an override means one thing in every condition and the screen always says the same true thing — your action stands. A mode switch keyed on connector health would flip the desk's meaning mid-shift on a false trigger, and connector health is exactly the signal R27 proved unreliable: a connector can be authenticated, polling and green while check-ins specifically have stopped arriving. The outage surfaces as a per-capability staleness banner that gates nothing.

Either way it takes the stay's write permission — the same permission that made the override. Author-only fails across shifts; supervisor-only escalates a routine reconciliation. There is no disagreement.clear permission (GUEST-Q3).
A disagreement is values that differ, and nothing else. That single definition is what turns S36's feared twenty reconciliations into the two that are real: when a six-hour outage ends and fourteen facts arrive at once, thirteen of them match what the desk already recorded and settle without anybody seeing them. No disagreement.* event is published — a disagreement is a fact about our records, not about the hotel, and ADR 0016 Part 2 says we publish the fact and not the process. The correction is the business fact, and it is a fact consumers already know how to handle.

The empty state that would be wrong here is the usual one. "No reservations yet — create your first booking", on a property with two thousand of them waiting in the inbox. The Hub took the honest option when this domain did not exist: normalise, store, and publish nothing — because the alternative was publishing onto a subject with no owner, where a dead letter is the best outcome and a silent drop the likely one.

The reference had four verbs downstream — check-in, check-out, change, update — and the branch that distinguished a change from an update was commented out (R8). Everything then arrived as an update, and housekeeping could not tell a vacated room from a corrected phone number. The design's answer is not more verbs: it is that the room lives in its own row, so a move is structurally a different operation from an amendment and cannot accidentally be published as one.

Every one of these ends at a person, and that is the design decision. The system this replaces had a single untyped dumbReason string holding both "a human must look" (NO RESERVATION ID) and "correctly superseded, keep for audit" (CHECKOUT SUCCESS) — so nobody could tell which rows needed attention, and in practice none of them got it (R26). The four outcomes the Hub types its failures into, and the Attention list here, are the same idea at two layers: a failure that cannot be told apart from a routine outcome is a failure nobody will ever work.

"Opera will not be told." Nothing GuestOps records reaches the PMS in v1 (CONN-Q5, ADR 0128 §4). A cancellation dialog that stayed silent about that would let a receptionist believe the room had been released in Opera — and the room would be sold twice. It is the one sentence on that screen that cannot be cut for tidiness.

And no missing neighbour may ever stop a flow — owner, 2026-08-31: an application's own flow is never gated on another application being installed; an absent dependency loses its capability, never the flow. So all three resolvers above are display-only. GuestOps has exactly one hard gate and it is its own fact: check-in refuses a stay with no room (S8). Room readiness never refuses anything — a cross-application gate would make an installable application effectively mandatory.
Both cross-application panels are drawn and not built — ratified, and slice 4 of the design is explicitly blocked on it. What is missing is not a decision but two contributing-domain read views and their Context RPCs, which belong to Jobs' and Room Care's own rounds, exactly as this application owns the three views that answer phone → guest → reservation → room. Until they exist the panels are absent, no data has to be migrated when they arrive, and nothing in a stay's history is orphaned when an application is uninstalled.
Deliberately not drawn.

The folio. Posting, payments, settlement, invoicing and the night-audit posting are Finance's later round (GUEST-Q6). The stay's commercial terms are in v1 and are drawn in the gold mockup's frame 7; the balance beside them is dashed, and the connector balance-fetch is recorded as a candidate capability for the connector's second round — it is a read, but v1's inbound contract excludes it.
Guest merge. The person-graph, suggested links and merges are Guest360's (G360-Q1). GuestOps holds guest_id; a merge re-points the person and rewrites no stay, which is exactly why no person_id is stored here.
Write-back of any kind. Not a check-in, not a room assignment, not a guest detail (CONN-Q5).
Availability and inventory. Nothing here answers "do we have a Deluxe King on 3 September?" — see the round's open-questions page; it is the largest gap the design knows about, and it is named rather than quietly assumed.
Room state. Clean, ready, inspected and who cleans are Room Care's; these flows announce occupancy and departure and assert nothing about what work that becomes (APPS-Q1).
Authorization tuples. ADR 0061's materialisation is unbuilt platform-wide; v1 needs no stay-level object, so it is named rather than depended on.
